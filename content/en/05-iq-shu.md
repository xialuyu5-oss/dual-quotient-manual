## How to practice a technique

Before the moves, the method. The main difference between a tool and a technique is that **a technique must be practiced, one at a time**. Practicing all twelve simultaneously is much like practicing none.

Choose one each month and put it where you will see it daily. Whenever its trigger appears, deliberately use it. In the evening, record how often you used it and what happened. After a month, it should begin appearing more naturally. Then move to the next.

The twelve fall into three groups: **seeing the problem clearly** (1–4), **calibrating judgment** (5–8), and **dealing with yourself** (9–12).

## Seeing the problem clearly

### 1. State the problem three ways

**Principle:** Most wrong solutions begin with the wrong question.
**Practice:** Before acting, restate the problem three ways: for yourself, for a nonspecialist, and for the most demanding expert. If you cannot make all three coherent, you do not yet understand the problem.
**Trigger:** Whenever you think, “I know how to do this.”
**Misuse:** Turning restatement into procrastination. After three versions, act.

### 2. First principles

**Principle:** Break the claim down to basic facts, then rebuild from them.
**Practice:** At each level of a conclusion, ask whether it is fact or inference. Continue until only facts you would stake something substantial on remain. Reason upward from there and see whether the original conclusion still follows.
**Trigger:** Saying “It has always been this way” or “Everyone does it.”
**Misuse:** Deriving everything from first principles. It is expensive; reserve it for important, unusual problems.

### 3. Inversion

**Principle:** As Munger advised, turn the problem around.
**Practice:** Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it. Instead of asking what to do, ask what to stop doing.
**Trigger:** Planning or feeling helpless.
**Misuse:** Only looking backward and becoming a habitual fault-finder. Inversion helps you avoid holes, not avoid walking.

### 4. Second-order thinking

**Principle:** And then what? And after that?
**Practice:** Trace at least three steps of consequences for an action. What will it cause? What will that cause? What happens next? Pay particular attention to how other people will respond.
**Trigger:** A proposal seems obviously beneficial and nobody objects.
**Misuse:** Following consequences so far that you become paralyzed. Three steps are enough.

## Calibrating judgment

### 5. Speak in percentages

**Principle:** Translate “I think so” into “I am 70% confident.”
**Practice:** Assign a probability to predictions and put it in the decision log. At year-end, check whether events you called 70% actually happened about 70% of the time.
**Trigger:** Sentences containing “should,” “probably” or “certainly.”
**Misuse:** Mistaking a number for precision. Its value is that it can be checked, not that it is inherently accurate.

### 6. Start with the base rate

**Principle:** Before looking at your case, look at what usually happens in comparable cases.
**Practice:** Ask what proportion of similar projects, startups, marriages or weight-loss plans succeed. Start there, then adjust for your circumstances—less than you are inclined to.
**Trigger:** Thinking “This time we are different.”
**Misuse:** Being imprisoned by the base rate. It is an anchor, not a cage.

### 7. Conduct a premortem

**Principle:** Assume the undertaking has already failed and write a report explaining why.
**Practice:** Before starting, ask everyone to write independently: “It is a year from now and this has failed. What most likely caused it?” Collect the answers to reveal risks more candidly than routine reassurance would.
**Trigger:** The launch of an important decision.
**Misuse:** Doing the premortem without changing the plan. The purpose is to improve the plan before starting.

### 8. Steelman the other side

**Principle:** Present the other person's argument better than they did.
**Practice:** Before rebutting it, state their position in its strongest form until they agree that you have understood. Munger's standard was that you should be able to explain the opposing argument better than its advocate before firmly holding the opposite view.
**Trigger:** Any time you want to say “but.”
**Misuse:** Treating steelmanning as a debating trick. Its purpose is to make changing your mind possible, not to make victory prettier.

## Dealing with yourself

### 9. The Feynman technique

**Principle:** If you cannot explain it clearly, you do not understand it.
**Practice:** Explain something you think you know in words a ten-year-old could understand, without jargon. Where you get stuck is where your understanding is missing. Return, learn, then explain again.
**Trigger:** After learning something or before teaching it.
**Misuse:** Assuming clear explanation means practical ability. Practice still separates understanding from doing.

### 10. Separate the decision from its outcome

**Principle:** A good decision can have a bad outcome, and a bad decision can have a good one.
**Practice:** In review, first ask whether the choice was reasonable given the information then available, then ask about the outcome. Evaluate them separately. Annie Duke calls confusing the two “resulting”: it teaches false lessons from bad luck and dangerous confidence from good luck.
**Trigger:** Every review.
**Misuse:** Using “The decision was sound” to evade responsibility for the outcome. Separate evaluation does not remove accountability.

### 11. Distinguish reversible from irreversible

**Principle:** Some doors let you return; others do not.
**Practice:** First ask whether a wrong choice can be undone and at what cost. Make reversible choices quickly, experiment and adjust. Slow down for irreversible choices and use the preceding techniques.
**Trigger:** The first moment of any decision.
**Misuse:** Treating everything as irreversible and making everything slow. Most decisions are reversible.

### 12. Pause

**Principle:** Notice when your reactions are taking over.
**Practice:** Learn your personal signals of a “System 1 takeover”: a racing heart, an urge to reply immediately, a need to prove something, the thought that the other person is stupid. When a signal appears, do nothing for ten minutes. For important matters, wait overnight.
**Trigger:** The signals themselves.
**Misuse:** Turning a pause into permanent indecision. Pause so that you can return.

## The limits of technique

Once the twelve are familiar, you will notice their shared ceiling: **they depend on your state**. Fatigue, anger, flattery and threats can disable them together. This is not simply insufficient practice; it is part of the nature of technique.

To move beyond the ceiling, turn techniques into something less dependent on your state. That is Fa: systems.
