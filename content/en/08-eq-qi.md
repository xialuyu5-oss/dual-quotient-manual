> — *The Doctrine of the Mean*

## Emotions are data

Clever people sometimes dismiss emotions as noise interfering with reason, best suppressed. Neuroscience does not support so simple a view.

Antonio Damasio studied patients with damage affecting emotional processing. Their intelligence tests and logical reasoning could remain normal, yet ordinary decisions became extraordinarily difficult. Scheduling an appointment could take half an hour because they had lost a way to care about the alternatives. Without emotional markers, every option looked much the same.

Emotions are data, not merely noise. Fear can signal risk; anger, a crossed boundary; shame, distance from a group's norms; pleasure, something that matters to you. The issue is not having feelings but **understanding their message and deciding whether to follow it**.

Emotional tools are instruments for reading that data.

## 1. An emotion vocabulary

Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett's work links more precise emotional distinctions with better regulation. Instead of merely saying you feel bad, distinguish irritation, feeling wronged, offense or frustrated resentment. Naming can begin the regulation process.

Keep a vocabulary within reach. Here is a starting point:

| Family | Words ranging from milder to stronger experiences |
| --- | --- |
| Anger | Displeased, irritated, annoyed, wronged, offended, resentful, furious, bitter |
| Fear | Uneasy, apprehensive, worried, anxious, panicked, frightened, despairing |
| Sadness | Disappointed, regretful, lonely, hurt, sad, heartbroken, numb |
| Joy | Grounded, reassured, relaxed, grateful, delighted, ecstatic |
| Shame and guilt | Awkward, embarrassed, guilty, ashamed, self-reproachful |
| Comparison | Admiring, envious, unwilling to concede, superior |
| Mixed | Grateful yet pressured; excited yet afraid; relieved yet a little disappointed |

Use it in one simple way: at least once daily, pause and select **the most accurate word** for the moment. Choose the precise word, not the easiest one.

## 2. Body scanning and breathing

Feelings often appear in the body before conscious recognition: a tight chest, clenched jaw, sweaty palms, a sinking stomach. Such signals can give you a few seconds' notice. Reading your body creates a small lead time.

**Body scan:** Close your eyes and move attention from head to feet, asking where you feel tension. Take thirty seconds.

**Breathing:** Two simple practices:
- A physiological sigh: two short inhalations, the second topping up the first, followed by a long exhalation. Repeat two or three times.
- Four-seven-eight: inhale for four seconds, hold for seven, exhale for eight. Repeat four times.

These do not make emotions disappear. They can help shift you from being pushed by a feeling to observing it. That difference underlies the techniques that follow.

## 3. An emotion journal

Make one entry daily in a fixed format:

**Trigger** (what happened) → **Body** (where I felt it) → **Emotion** (name it precisely) → **Thought** (the sentence in my head) → **Behavior** (what I did) → **Need** (what I actually wanted).

After a month, reread it. Patterns emerge: similar triggers, bodily responses, thoughts and behaviors. Seeing a pattern comes before changing it, and begins the self-knowledge discussed in Chapter 11.

## 4. A pause button

There is a gap between stimulus and response, and our freedom lies in that gap. This idea is often attributed to Viktor Frankl; whatever the precise attribution, it captures the point of pausing.

The pause button deliberately lengthens the gap. It can take several forms:

- A physical action: drink water, stand up, turn your phone face down.
- A fixed sentence: “Let me think about that; I will reply later.”
- A rule: do not reply within ten minutes to any message that makes your heart race.

An emotion's initial physiological peak may subside within a minute or two. The pause is meant to help you get through that initial surge without acting automatically.

## 5. A needs list

A central insight in Marshall Rosenberg's nonviolent communication is that **a need lies behind a feeling**. Anger may point to respect, anxiety to certainty, hurt to being seen.

Emotion is the signal; need is its content. A needs list helps translate one into the other:

Respect · Being seen · Understanding · Safety · Autonomy · Belonging · Fairness · Certainty · Meaning · Growth · Rest · Trust · Contribution · Intimacy · Space

After naming a strong feeling, ask which need it speaks for. Finding the need moves you from “I am angry” toward “I need respect.” The latter offers something concrete to communicate; the former may only erupt.

## 6. A relationship ledger

List the ten to fifteen most important people in your life. For each, periodically note two things: **your latest deposit**—specific appreciation, a kept promise, attentive listening—and **your latest withdrawal**—a broken promise, harsh words, neglect.

Clever people often assume relationships are something one has rather than something one does. A ledger can reveal that someone you feel close to has received no deposit for three months.

## Three principles

**1. Tools help you see, not eliminate.** Their purpose is not to remove feelings but to let you see clearly while feeling them. Never experiencing anger is not automatically emotional maturity; it may be a lack of sensitivity.

**2. Use them on yourself before using them on others.** Begin with your own vocabulary, body, journal and needs. Without reading yourself, attempts to read others can become projection.

**3. Record, then review.** As with thinking tools, emotional notes that are never revisited become merely a tidier complaint book.

## From tools to techniques

When you can identify feeling wronged rather than angry without consulting a chart, pause at the first tightening in your chest without a reminder, and ask what you really need even in anger without a checklist, tools recede and technique appears.
