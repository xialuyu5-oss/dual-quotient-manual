## How to practice

As in Chapter 5: **one technique at a time, a new one each month**. Record daily in your emotion journal how often you used it, in what setting and with what effect.

Emotional techniques add a difficulty: their triggers usually arrive when you are at your worst—offended, rejected or misunderstood. Accept in advance that **early attempts will often fail**. Remembering afterward is already progress.

The twelve form three groups: **yourself** (1–4), **others** (5–8), and **conflict** (9–12).

## Working with yourself

### 1. Name it

**Principle:** Naming a feeling can make it feel smaller.
**Practice:** As emotion rises, label it inwardly with a precise word: “This is offense,” or “This is frustrated resentment.” Do not judge or explain; just name. Research on affect labeling has measured reduced amygdala activation in some settings.
**Trigger:** Any bodily signal of emotion.
**Misuse:** Immediately analyzing why you feel this way. First name it; save analysis for the evening journal.

### 2. Wait three seconds

**Principle:** Three seconds can help turn a reaction into a response.
**Practice:** Before speaking, silently count to three. Nothing else is required. Just wait.
**Trigger:** The urge to rebut, explain or reply immediately.
**Misuse:** Pausing only to aim your counterattack. The pause should admit other possibilities, not improve your aim.

### 3. Translate the need

**Principle:** Translate “I am furious” into “I need …”.
**Practice:** After naming an emotion, ask which need it speaks for. Use Chapter 8's needs list. Once found, state the need to yourself or the other person.
**Trigger:** After naming any strong feeling.
**Misuse:** Treating your need as a demand the other person must fulfill. The need is yours; the other person retains a choice.

### 4. Reframe

**Principle:** There is always another way to describe the same event.
**Practice:** Write three accounts of something upsetting: “They did not reply because they disrespect me”; “They may be busy”; “They are not good at prompt replies, and it may have nothing to do with me.” You need not believe any one account. Notice that your first interpretation is not the only one.
**Trigger:** Drawing conclusions with “definitely,” “must” or “obviously.”
**Misuse:** Using reframing to deny your feelings. It changes interpretation, not the legitimacy of having a feeling.

## Working with others

### 5. Reflect back

**Principle:** Before responding, show that you heard.
**Practice:** Restate the content and feeling in your own words, ending with “Do you mean …?” Wait for confirmation or correction. Only then begin your response.
**Trigger:** After the other person finishes a substantial point in an important conversation.
**Misuse:** Sounding like an interrogator or someone applying a trick. Be genuinely curious.

### 6. Ask three perspective-taking questions

**Principle:** What do they fear? What do they want? What does the world look like to them?
**Practice:** When someone puzzles or upsets you, seriously answer all three until each answer makes their position understandable.
**Trigger:** Wanting to say, “How could they do that?”
**Misuse:** Abandoning your own position after understanding theirs. Understanding is not agreement.

### 7. Four steps of nonviolent communication

**Principle:** Observation, feeling, need, request—without judgment or accusation.
**Practice:** “When you [specific behavior], I feel [emotion], because I need [need]. Would you be willing to [specific feasible request]?” The observation should be concrete enough to record on video; the feeling precise; the need your own; and the request something the other person can decline.
**Trigger:** Expressing dissatisfaction or making a request.
**Misuse:** Reciting a template mechanically. With practice, the four elements become natural speech rather than a script.

### 8. Give feedback

**Principle:** Situation, behavior, impact—three statements without character judgment.
**Practice:** “At yesterday's review meeting [situation], you took over before she finished [behavior]. I noticed she did not speak again afterward [impact].” Then stop. Do not add “You always do this” or “You should.” Let the facts speak.
**Trigger:** Any urge to say, “The kind of person you are …”.
**Misuse:** Using SBI as packaging for veiled criticism. Its purpose is precision, not camouflage.

## Working with conflict

### 9. Receive feedback

**Principle:** Thank, ask, digest; do not immediately defend.
**Practice:** Start with “Thank you for saying that.” Ask a clarifying question such as “Can you give an example?” Then say you need time to think. Even if you believe the criticism is wrong, avoid an instant defense and respond after a day.
**Trigger:** Being criticized or contradicted.
**Misuse:** Thanking and then forgetting. Reflection is essential; without it, the opening is performance.

### 10. Cool the exchange

**Principle:** Lower your volume, slow down, acknowledge a genuine point.
**Practice:** As conflict heats up, speak a little more quietly and slowly than the other person. Find a small part of what they said that you honestly agree with and say so.
**Trigger:** Either person's voice begins to rise.
**Misuse:** Confusing partial agreement with total surrender. You can acknowledge one point and maintain the rest of your position.

### 11. Repair

**Principle:** Relationship quality depends not only on conflict but on how repair happens.
**Practice:** John Gottman's research highlights repair attempts in stable relationships. Within twenty-four hours of conflict, return with three elements: **acknowledge** (“I interrupted you yesterday”), **recognize impact** (“That may have left you feeling disrespected”), and **change** (“Next time I will wait until you finish”). Do not add “but.”
**Trigger:** After a conflict ends.
**Misuse:** Trading an apology for forgiveness. Repair is your action; the other person's response is not yours to control.

### 12. Say no

**Principle:** Be clear, brief and sparing with explanations.
**Practice:** “I cannot do that.” Stop. If asked, give one reason. Offer an alternative if you have one: “I can …”. Do not apologize three times, invent excuses or leave false openings such as “I'll see.”
**Trigger:** Saying yes aloud while saying no inwardly.
**Misuse:** Saying no to project toughness. Its purpose is honesty, not intimidation.

## The limits of technique

Once these moves are familiar, the same ceiling as in Chapter 5 appears: **they depend on your state**. Fatigue, old wounds or a conversation with someone you care about deeply can disable them.

They also carry a special danger: **skill can become manipulation**. You know how to make people comfortable, obtain agreement and defuse objections—but why? If the answer is to win, emotional skill is sliding toward power play.

Neither reliability nor direction is settled at the technique level. Systems address the former; understanding and character address the latter.
