> Build a solid camp; fight the unglamorous battle.
> — Zeng Guofan

## The nature of systems

Technique is a move; a system is a routine. A move relies on someone choosing to use it. A routine makes using it part of the arrangement.

The central question is: **Can you still do the right thing on your worst day?** If not, every capability has a large gap. Important moments often arrive when your state is poor: tired, provoked, flattered or pressured to answer immediately.

The design principle is therefore: **do not overtrust your future self**. Assume that person will be more impulsive, lazy and self-deceiving than you are now, and lay tracks in advance.

Zeng Guofan did not rely chiefly on brilliant stratagems. His maxim was to build solid camps and fight patiently: dig ditches and build walls on arriving, then advance gradually. This is not stupidity but a system. Inspiration-dependent tricks are fragile; solid foundations are not.

Here are six systems and one manual.

## 1. Feedback loops

Practice without feedback does not produce improvement. If you do the same task for ten years without learning whether it worked, you have repeated for ten years, not practiced for ten years.

For each capability you want to improve, create a loop: **act → record expectations → observe results → compare → adjust**. Omit any link and the loop breaks.

Clever people's loops most often break at recording expectations. Without a record, they can always claim afterward that they knew all along. The decision log in Chapter 4 repairs this link.

## 2. A review routine

Three rhythms are needed:

**Daily review, five minutes:** Which judgment would I revise? Which interaction do I regret or appreciate? What did I learn?

**Weekly review, thirty minutes:** Read the week's decision log and notes. Check whether last week's planned adjustments happened. Write next week's single most important task.

**Quarterly review, half a day:** Revisit every prediction due for checking this quarter and calculate accuracy. Repeat the self-assessment. Ask the larger question: over these three months, have I become merely cleverer or also wiser?

The military after-action review is commonly organized around four questions: What was expected? What happened? Why the difference? What should happen next time? These questions work at any scale.

The central rule is **fixed time, fixed form, independent of mood**. When you feel good, review seems unnecessary; when you feel bad, it seems unwelcome. Systems exist for both situations.

## 3. Decision categories

Not every choice deserves slow deliberation. Classify decisions on two axes: **Is it reversible? Is the impact large?**

| | Small impact | Large impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reversible** | Act immediately; do not overthink | Act quickly, but use the decision log |
| **Irreversible** | Think briefly and ask someone | Use the twelve techniques, wait overnight, write a premortem |

The main value is not slowing important choices but **speeding unimportant ones**, reserving cognitive resources for the bottom-right cell.

## 4. A learning cycle

Learning is a four-part loop: **input → processing → output → feedback**. Most people stop at input.

- **Input:** Read, listen, watch.
- **Processing:** Restate in your own words, connect to what you know, seek counterexamples.
- **Output:** Write, explain to someone, make something.
- **Feedback:** Let the world tell you whether the output is sound.

Set a rule: **without output, learning is unfinished**. Finish a book and write a page. Learn a method and teach it once. Understand a model and use it on a real situation.

## 5. A knowledge map

Draw three circles: **what I know**, **what I know I do not know**, and **what I think I know but do not**.

The third is the most dangerous and often the largest for clever people. Its boundary can be found through reality contradicting you or through another person challenging you. Deliberately arrange the latter: regularly ask a knowledgeable person where your thinking is mistaken, and pay a real cost—in time, money or pride—to listen.

Update the map each quarter. Has the third circle shrunk?

## 6. Cognitive hygiene

Thinking is a physiological activity. These practices are as simple as brushing your teeth, and often neglected:

- **Information diet:** Manage both volume and quality. Much daily information is noise; enough noise drowns out the signal.
- **Sleep:** Lack of sleep can impair judgment in ways comparable to intoxication. Avoid important decisions when sleep-deprived.
- **Timing:** Find your two clearest hours of the day and put the hardest judgments there.
- **Two prohibitions:** Do not decide in anger; do not promise in excitement.

## A principles manual

After the six systems have operated for a while, you will notice recurring situations and recurring good responses. Write them down as **“When …, I …”**.

That is your principles manual. Ray Dalio's method is to extract a principle from each painful experience: pain plus reflection yields progress. You do not need hundreds. Twenty or thirty can cover much of what repeatedly arises in your life.

A principles manual is a concentrated form of Fa. It compresses tools and techniques into usable instructions and speaks for you when your state is worst.

## The trap of systems

Systems have their own characteristic failure: **attachment to rules**. The system works well, and you begin serving it for its own sake, feeling uneasy about anything outside the process and forgetting its original purpose.

The remedy is not found in more rules but in Dao. The next chapter turns there.
