> A craftsperson who would do good work must first sharpen the tools.
> — *Analects*, “Duke Ling of Wei”

## What tools are—and are not

Tools extend thinking: they enlarge memory, constrain impulses and record judgments for later comparison. They do not think for you. However good your notes, if you never revisit them, your understanding is no greater than that of someone who never takes notes.

Clever people are particularly drawn to tools because they offer immediate mastery. Installing new software or learning a framework makes you feel stronger. That feeling is an illusion. A tool gains value through repeated use, and repetition is boring. This chapter therefore follows one rule: **few and good, each with a clear trigger for when to use it**.

These six can serve for a lifetime.

## 1. External memory

A searchable note system has only three requirements: **one entrance**, so everything goes to one place rather than five apps; **retrievability**, so you can find a six-month-old note in thirty seconds; and **regular review**, once a week for the week's notes and once a quarter for the quarter's.

The third requirement matters most and is the one nearly everyone neglects. Notes without review are merely a tidier rubbish heap.

**When to use it:** Whenever you think “That is interesting; I should note it”—and during your scheduled weekly review.

## 2. Checklists

In *The Checklist Manifesto*, surgeon Atul Gawande describes how a preoperative checklist can substantially reduce deaths. The issue is not that doctors lack knowledge, but that even very intelligent people overlook obvious steps under pressure.

Write one checklist for each recurring high-stakes situation: an important email, a crucial presentation, a contract, or negative feedback to someone. Keep each to no more than seven items.

**When to use it:** Whenever you enter the relevant situation, however confident you feel. Checklists are precisely for the moments when you feel sure.

## 3. A decision log

This is the chapter's most important tool. For every important decision, record five things:

1. What the decision is.
2. Why you chose it: the information and reasoning available at the time.
3. What you expect to happen.
4. How confident you are, as a percentage.
5. When you will return to check.

The log counters a major enemy of clever people: **hindsight rationalization**. Without a log, memory quietly edits yesterday's reasons to make you look consistently wise. With one, you can finally see how accurate your judgments were. The number is usually humbling.

**When to use it:** For a decision whose effects last more than a month and that cannot easily be reversed.

## 4. A library of mental models

This is Charlie Munger's latticework of mental models: a few dozen fundamentals borrowed across disciplines—compounding, margins, regression to the mean, incentives, feedback loops, bottlenecks, base rates, survivorship bias and more.

A model library remains a tool until used; only fluent use makes it a technique. Most people's libraries are lists they never apply. The only way to make one live is to **examine each situation through at least two models**.

**When to use it:** When analyzing any moderately complex situation, begin by asking which models are operating within it.

## 5. A container for time

Deep thought requires uninterrupted time. That is a physical condition, not an attitude. Reserve at least one ninety-minute container daily: no messages, no calls, only the task that requires real thought.

Clever people often overestimate their ability to think anywhere, anytime. After repeated interruptions, thought often does little more than repeat ideas already held.

**When to use it:** Every day. Treat it as a meeting that cannot be canceled.

## 6. A collection of good questions

Keep a list of questions you use repeatedly. Good questions are the lightest tools and among the sharpest. Examples:

- If this judgment is wrong, how is it most likely wrong?
- Who bears the consequences here, and who does not?
- How would I explain this to a ten-year-old in one sentence?
- How will my self five years from now see today's choice?
- Am I trying to win or to get it right?
- What would the smartest person who disagrees with me say?

Add useful questions; remove those you have not used for three months.

**When to use it:** When stuck—and when everything seems too easy.

## Three principles for using tools

**1. Few and good.** Keep one tool of each kind. Two note systems can be as ineffective as having none.

**2. Every tool needs a trigger.** Knowing when matters more than knowing how. Without a trigger, a tool is used only when it happens to come to mind, which is often too late.

**3. Tools must not replace thinking.** Recording is not understanding, collecting is not mastery, and a model library is not insight. The test of a tool is whether it has changed an actual decision you made.

## From tools to techniques

When you know what to check without looking at the checklist, recognize regression to the mean without opening the model library, and ask how confident you are without opening the decision log, the tool recedes and technique appears.
