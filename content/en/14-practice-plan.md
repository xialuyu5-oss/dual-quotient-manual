> Read one book at a time: until one is finished, do not begin another.
> — Zeng Guofan, “Twelve Daily Rules”

## Principles of the plan

Three principles matter more than every item that follows.

**1. Consistency, not volume.** Zeng Guofan's rule of one book at a time points to the real enemy: not merely laziness but greed, trying to practice everything and mastering nothing. The daily routine below should fit within roughly thirty minutes. If that is too much, reduce it to ten, but do it every day.

**2. Adjust priorities to your stage.** The table below specifies each stage's emphasis. Do not replace the work of discovering tools with abstract contemplation, or the reverse.

**3. Keep a record.** After three months, memory can polish unrecorded practice into “I have been doing it all along.” Use the Practice page or a sheet of paper.

## Daily practice: about thirty minutes

### Three morning questions: five minutes

After waking, before checking your phone, write answers to:

1. What is today's most important judgment? Mark it in advance as deserving slower thought.
2. Who or what is most likely to stir my emotions? Prepare the pause button.
3. What kind of person do I want to be today? One word is enough: patient, honest, present.

### Name one emotion: anytime

At least once, pause and use the most precise word in your vocabulary for the current feeling. Ask which need it represents and record it in your emotion journal.

### One decision-log entry: anytime

Record at least one decision: what, why, expected outcome, confidence and review date. Small decisions count. Keep the recording habit active.

### Read a passage: fifteen minutes

Read a classic or a genuinely worthwhile book for fifteen minutes, without rushing. Finish one before starting another. Write one sentence capturing today's most important point.

### Evening review: five minutes

Ask three questions, followed by Zengzi's three examinations:

1. Which judgment would I revise?
2. Which interaction do I regret or appreciate?
3. What did I learn?

Was I conscientious in helping others? Trustworthy with friends? Practicing what I learned?

## Weekly practice: about one hour, preferably Sunday

- **Review the logs:** Read decision and emotion entries. Look for patterns, not faults.
- **Review relationship accounts:** Make a deposit for the person who has gone longest without one.
- **Have a deep conversation:** At least thirty minutes with one person, without an agenda, truly listening.
- **Practice inversion:** Write the opposing case to your strongest conviction of the week until it seems genuinely plausible.
- **Review this month's technique:** How often did you use it, and what happened?
- **Name next week's single most important task:** Write it down. One task.

## Monthly practice: half a day

- **Change the technique:** Has the month's move begun to appear automatically? If yes, select the next. If not, continue for another month.
- **Produce an output:** Write a page about a book you finished or teach someone a method you learned.
- **Repair one thing:** An unrepaired relationship, an overdue apology or unspoken gratitude.
- **Update the principles manual:** Did a painful experience yield a useful “When …, I …” principle?

## Quarterly practice: half a day alone

- **Repeat the self-assessment:** Compare the shape with the preceding quarter, not just the scores.
- **Check predictions:** Review all predictions due this quarter. Of those you called 70% likely, how many happened?
- **Update the knowledge map:** Has the circle of things you thought you knew but did not become smaller?
- **Clear space:** Which techniques have become natural and can leave the practice list? Which tools have gone unused for three months?
- **Step back:** Spend half a day alone without input and ask what all this is for.

## Yearly practice

- **Write a letter** to yourself one year from now: your most important judgment this year, its expected result, and the pattern you most want to change. Open it next year and compare.
- **Reread the overview:** Appendix B's one-page outline. Which lines read differently now?
- **Update your principles:** Remove what is no longer needed and add what the year taught you.

## Priorities for the four stages

| Stage | Thinking path | Emotional path | Shared practice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Discover tools** | Choose one note system; begin the decision log | Use emotion vocabulary; begin the emotion journal | Add nothing new for three months |
| **Practice techniques** | One per month, starting with pausing | One per month, starting with naming | Keep the daily evening review |
| **Establish systems** | Three review rhythms, decision categories, principles | Relationship accounts, repair mechanisms, conflict agreements | Follow the system for a year regardless of mood |
| **Cultivate understanding** | Teach, take responsibility, step back regularly | Practice with difficult people and conversations | Return to tools and become a student again |

If you are unsure of your stage, take the self-assessment.

## Three reminders

**You will miss days.** Everyone does. A break is not failure; never returning is. Resume the next day without catching up or blaming yourself.

**Progress may be invisible.** Character changes over years. You may never wake up feeling suddenly wise. Instead, one year you notice that things which once overwhelmed you have not done so for a long time.

**The manual may become outdated; you need not.** A year from now, many items may no longer fit. That is a good sign. Keep what helps, remove the rest and add your own. By then, you are writing your own manual.
