> Even a wise person's thousand considerations contain a mistake.
> — *Records of the Grand Historian*, “Biography of the Marquis of Huaiyin”

## Why devote a chapter to traps?

Many mistakes arise from insufficient ability. Clever people's characteristic mistakes often arise **from their abilities themselves**. Strong reasoning enables rationalization, logic wins arguments, quick understanding breeds impatience. More cleverness alone therefore cannot reliably prevent these traps and may deepen them.

Each of the following twelve has a remedy and a reference to its place in the manual. Copy the three you fall into most often and put them somewhere visible.

## 1. Rationalization

**Symptoms:** Within seconds you can produce five reasons for any decision already made and find a weakness in every objection. You almost never genuinely change your mind.

**Remedy:** Write reasons and expectations before deciding, so your later self cannot rewrite the earlier one. Ask how you are most likely wrong.

**Where:** Chapter 4, decision log; Chapter 5, technique 7, premortem.

## 2. Winning arguments

**Symptoms:** Every discussion quietly becomes a contest. You win repeatedly, then discover people no longer discuss things with you.

**Remedy:** Before speaking, ask whether you want to win or get it right. Steelman the other view before challenging it.

**Where:** Chapter 5, technique 8; Chapter 12, the two selves.

## 3. Speed

**Symptoms:** Speed proves your intelligence: fast replies, decisions and conclusions. If wrong, hurry again.

**Remedy:** Classify decisions. Move quickly on reversible choices and slowly on irreversible ones. Recognize your automatic-reaction signals and wait ten minutes when they appear.

**Where:** Chapter 5, techniques 11 and 12; Chapter 6, decision categories.

## 4. Impatience

**Symptoms:** Little patience for slow people, slow processes or slow returns. Constantly seeking shortcuts.

**Remedy:** Treat patience as a trainable skill rather than a fixed personality trait. Understand compounding: steady progress in the right direction can beat speed in the wrong one.

**Where:** Chapter 7, the long view; Chapter 14, consistency.

## 5. Arrogance

**Symptoms:** Cleverness defines your worth. Admitting ignorance, accepting correction or doing an unglamorous task threatens your identity.

**Remedy:** Anchor worth less in surpassing others and more in surpassing yesterday's self. Regularly do something at which you are a complete beginner.

**Where:** Chapter 7, four turns; Chapter 13, becoming a student again.

## 6. The illusion of knowledge

**Symptoms:** Reading becomes equivalent to ability, collecting to mastery, naming to understanding.

**Remedy:** Use the Feynman technique: inability to explain reveals a gap. Establish the rule that learning without output is unfinished.

**Where:** Chapter 5, technique 9; Chapter 6, learning cycle.

## 7. Emotional blindness

**Symptoms:** Treating feelings as noise to suppress, bypass or analyze away. They reappear elsewhere—in the body, relationships or unexplained exhaustion.

**Remedy:** Treat emotions as data. Name the feeling, identify the need and then decide whether to follow its signal.

**Where:** Chapter 8, emotions as data; Chapter 9, techniques 1 and 3.

## 8. Perfectionism

**Symptoms:** Seeing every flaw makes you delay delivery or exhaust yourself on unimportant details.

**Remedy:** Herbert Simon's satisficing: most tasks need to be good enough. Reserve the most demanding deliberation for high-impact, irreversible choices.

**Where:** Chapter 6, decision categories.

## 9. Resulting

**Symptoms:** Judging decisions by outcomes alone. Good luck creates confidence; bad luck creates self-blame. Both can teach the wrong lesson.

**Remedy:** Evaluate decision quality separately from outcome. State probabilities and check calibration over time.

**Where:** Chapter 5, techniques 5 and 10.

## 10. Persuasion

**Symptoms:** Believing clear enough explanation must produce agreement. If it does not, repeat the argument more clearly.

**Remedy:** People feel as well as think. Connect before correcting. Until emotional intensity settles, logic may be received as noise.

**Where:** Chapter 12, crucial conversations.

## 11. Hoarding

**Symptoms:** A hundred apps, fifty frameworks, twenty unfinished books. The feeling of progress from collecting replaces progress itself.

**Remedy:** One in, one out. For every added tool, remove another. Spend three months using what you already know rather than adding new material.

**Where:** Chapter 4, usage principles; Chapter 13, the comfort of tools.

## 12. Fixed self-stories

**Symptoms:** “That is just who I am.” “I am naturally bad with people.” “I have always been impatient.” A definition pins you in place.

**Remedy:** Carol Dweck's growth-mindset approach: replace “I am …” with “At present, I have not yet developed …”. Observe patterns in the emotion journal rather than defining yourself by labels.

**Where:** Chapter 11, clarity; Chapter 8, emotion journal.

## The common structure

Each trap **uses a strength where it does not belong**.

Reasoning used only for self-defense becomes rationalization. Logic used to overpower becomes argument-winning. Speed applied to irreversible decisions becomes the speed trap. Learning ability spent on collection becomes hoarding.

The remedy is not to weaken the strength but to **give it direction and brakes**. Understanding supplies direction; systems supply brakes. That is the manual's central theme.
